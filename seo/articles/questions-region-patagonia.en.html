Yes: Argentine and Chilean Patagonia is a safe destination for the vast majority of travelers who take basic precautions. Official travel advice for Argentina is at the normal level: Canada asks for "normal security precautions" and flags regional advisories only for Greater Buenos Aires and Rosario, and the UK has no current travel warnings. For Chile, Canada asks for "a high degree of caution" nationwide because of demonstrations, civil unrest and crime, and the UK's regional warning covers the Araucanía, which is not Patagonia. The risks that come up most often in Patagonia are theft from cars and bus terminals, weather and mountain conditions, and a few health risks (hantavirus, sun, stream water). Preparation avoids almost all of them.

What official travel advice says

  • Argentina, Canada: "Take normal security precautions (with regional advisories)". It asks for "a high degree of caution" only in Greater Buenos Aires and Rosario. No Patagonian region has its own advisory. Updated October 14, 2026 (Government of Canada).
  • Argentina, United Kingdom: no travel warnings on the "Warnings and insurance" page, which was still current on November 25, 2026 (last updated May 7, 2026, with new information on hantavirus) (FCDO).
  • Chile, Canada: "Exercise a high degree of caution" nationwide because of ongoing demonstrations, civil unrest and crime. Updated November 13, 2026 (Government of Canada).
  • Chile, United Kingdom: the regional warning covers the Araucanía and the provinces of Biobío, Arauco, Cautín and Malleco, none of which is Patagonia. Updated November 9, 2026 (FCDO).

Both governments agree on the main point: the typical problem for a tourist is petty crime, not violence against visitors.

Urban crime

The main risk is non-violent theft: pickpockets, snatched bags and phones, and items stolen from cars.

  • Rental cars: the UK warns that thefts from unattended vehicles, "especially hire cars", are common across the country and "happen frequently in Bariloche" (FCDO Argentina, safety). Leave nothing visible, including at viewpoint parking areas.
  • Bus terminals and public transport: pickpockets operate on buses and at terminals (same UK advice). Keep cash and your passport close to your body.
  • Chile: the UK says pickpocketing, mugging and thefts from vehicles are "very common throughout Chile", including airports, bus stations, viewpoints and petrol stations (FCDO Chile, safety). Canada lists lookouts, bus stations and airports as frequent theft spots (Government of Canada).
  • If you are robbed: the UK advises handing over cash and valuables without resistance.
  • Women hiking alone: Canada reports that women hiking and cycling alone have been assaulted in Argentina (Government of Canada). Tell someone your route and, if you can, go with company.

Wildlife

Argentina's National Parks Administration (APN) states that parks are "wild areas" where there are risks from wild animals, weather, terrain and toxic plants (APN, visitor recommendations, in Spanish).

  • Big cats (puma): in parks that report their presence, the APN asks you to hike in groups and look after children. If you meet one: do not approach it, back away slowly, stay calm, do not run, and tell the nearest park ranger (APN, big cats).
  • Do not feed wildlife: it is prohibited. Human food changes animals' diet and behavior (APN, Los Glaciares). In Torres del Paine, Chile's CONAF also bans feeding native wildlife and bringing pets (CONAF).
  • Wasps and bees: the APN points to the yellowjacket (Vespula germanica), which lands on food outdoors. People who are allergic can have a serious reaction to a single sting: if that is you, see a doctor before traveling and bring your medication (APN, insects).
  • Rodents: they are the most serious health risk in the region; see the health section.

Weather and roads

  • Weather can change from one moment to the next, at any time of year. The APN says so for Los Glaciares and it applies across the region. Check the National Weather Service forecast and road conditions before you set out (APN, alerts). The SMN issues color-coded alerts (yellow, orange and red), including strong-wind alerts for Patagonia (SMN, alerts). In Chile, forecasts come from the Dirección Meteorológica de Chile.
  • Winter roads: in Los Glaciares, studded tires or approved chains are mandatory when there is snow, frost or ice, and the maximum speed on park roads is 40 km/h (APN, Los Glaciares). On mountain roads in northern Patagonia the limit is also 40 km/h (APN, Northern Patagonia, Dec 17, 2021).
  • Storms, trees and water: the APN recommends avoiding trails in strong wind because of falling trees and branches, getting out of the water in an electrical storm, and being careful in streams, since lakes and rivers are cold and deep and currents are dangerous (APN, natural hazards). Walking on frozen lagoons is prohibited (APN, Los Glaciares).
  • Fire: the APN rates wildfire danger with an index posted on signs, and fires are allowed only in designated places (APN, fire). Canada warns that high temperatures can lead to large fires across Argentina and that smoke can worsen air quality; in Chile, wildfires are common from December to March, mostly between Valparaíso and the Araucanía (Government of Canada). In Chile, CONAF is the agency that reports and coordinates.
  • Volcanoes and earthquakes in Chile: the UK notes a chain of active volcanoes in the Puyehue and Los Lagos area, with a continuing risk of eruptions at Chaitén, and that major earthquakes are a risk throughout Chile (FCDO Chile). The technical agency is SERNAGEOMIN and the emergency agency is SENAPRED.

Trails and mountains

  • Trekking registration: the APN treats it as mandatory and asks you to get information from rangers, respect hiking times and always walk in daylight (APN, hiking). In northern Patagonia parks it asks you to register at least 48 hours ahead (APN, Dec 17, 2021).
  • El Chaltén (Los Glaciares, north zone): to climb or enter remote areas you must register at the Visitor Center or online. There are no shops on the trails: carry water, food, extra layers, a flashlight and sunscreen. The APN recommends going with company and planning to be back in town before dark. From May to September trails have ice, mud and snow, and poles and crampons are mandatory.
  • Torres del Paine (Chile): CONAF asks you to buy your ticket at pasesparques.cl, check the weather and the right clothing, get information from official channels (CONAF, pasesparques.cl and parquetorresdelpaine.cl), stay out of rivers and lakes, and use a camp stove only where it is allowed: those who break the rules are expelled from the park (CONAF).
  • Signal and emergencies: mobile service in national parks is limited, and the APN asks you to find out each protected area's emergency number (APN, communication).
  • Water and food: river and stream water may not be drinkable, and you should not eat wild fruit or plants (APN, hiking).

Health

  • Hantavirus: it is a disease carried by wild rodents. Infection happens mainly by breathing particles from their urine, saliva or droppings, when entering their habitat: wild areas, rural settings and closed spaces such as sheds or mountain huts. Argentina's Ministry of Health notes there is also evidence of person-to-person transmission. Early symptoms look like the flu (fever above 38 °C, muscle pain, chills, headache, nausea and vomiting), and early care is key. If you develop a fever after sleeping in a hut, shed or closed cabin, see a doctor right away and say you were in rodent territory. The UK added hantavirus information for Argentina to its May 7, 2026 update and mentions it for Chile too (Ministry of Health, in Spanish; FCDO).
    The Ministry's prevention advice: air out for at least 30 minutes and wear an N95 mask before entering closed huts, sheds or houses in rural or wild areas, camp away from brush and garbage dumps, do not sleep directly on the ground, and drink safe water (2 drops of bleach per liter, let it stand 30 minutes, or boil it for 2 to 3 minutes).
  • Sun: the APN asks you to take care between 10 a.m. and 4 p.m., with sunglasses, a hat and SPF 30 or higher, reapplied every two hours (APN).
  • Avian flu in Chile: CONAF reports an outbreak of highly pathogenic avian influenza and publishes prevention measures on its Torres del Paine page (CONAF).
  • Vaccines: the UK recommends checking vaccines at least 8 weeks before you travel (FCDO). For your own country, ask your health authority.

Medical coverage for travelers

Argentina changed the rules for care of foreigners who are not permanent residents. This is what can be stated from official sources:

  • Emergencies are not conditioned. Decree 366/2025 (Official Gazette, May 29, 2025) states that in emergencies, access to health care cannot be denied or restricted to any foreigner, whatever their immigration status (Boletín Oficial, in Spanish).
  • Outside emergencies, in national-government hospitals: the same decree says foreigners without permanent residence receive routine care only on presenting health insurance or paying for the service in advance. Resolution 1066/2026 of the Ministry of Health (Official Gazette, August 11, 2026, in force from the next day) approved the procedure to apply it in facilities run by the national government (Boletín Oficial; Ministry of Health). The resolution covers national-government facilities: it says nothing about provincial or municipal hospitals.
  • Is insurance required at the border? Decree 366/2025 provides that foreigners must submit, on entry, a sworn statement that they hold health insurance, but the Migration Law states that this declaration "will be required once its implementation is regulated" (art. 123 bis) (updated text of Law 25,871, in Spanish). We could not confirm in official sources that it is required at entry today: the UK's entry-requirements page, updated May 7, 2026, mentions no mandatory insurance (FCDO, entry) and we did not find the requirement on the Migraciones page. Confirm with Migraciones or an Argentine consulate before you travel.
  • Chile: we found no equivalent requirement. The UK warns that private clinics are expensive and recommends insurance covering treatment and repatriation (FCDO Chile, health).

Even if it is not mandatory in your case, the UK recommends traveling with insurance that covers local treatment and medical evacuation, and its Argentina advice notes that medical facilities are good but can be expensive and that public hospitals tend to be crowded (FCDO Argentina, health). In the mountains an evacuation can take a long time: check that your policy covers trekking.

What to do before and during your trip

  1. Buy travel insurance with medical and evacuation cover, and confirm it covers the activities you plan.
  2. Check your own government's travel advice and the links in the "Sources" section.
  3. Check the forecast (SMN in Argentina, Dirección Meteorológica in Chile) and road conditions the day before and the same day.
  4. Register with the park before hiking, tell someone your route and return time, and set out in daylight.
  5. Leave nothing visible in the car; carry a copy of your passport.
  6. Write down each park's emergency number (the Visitor Center gives it) and the ones listed below.
  7. If you get a fever or flu-like symptoms after being in rodent territory, see a doctor immediately.

Agencies and phone numbers

Argentina (argentina.gob.ar, emergencies):

  • 911: national emergency center (ambulance or police).
  • 100: fire department.
  • 103: Civil Defense.
  • 106: boating emergencies on rivers, lakes and coasts (Prefectura Naval Argentina).
  • 107: SAME, medical emergencies. The official portal says the service is available in Buenos Aires City and localities of Buenos Aires province; in Patagonia, call 911 or the local service number.

In national parks, the emergency number is each protected area's own: ask at the Visitor Center. Agencies: National Parks Administration, National Weather Service, National Directorate of Migration.

Chile (Chile en el Exterior portal):

  • 131: SAMU, ambulances.
  • 132: fire department.
  • 133: Carabineros (police).
  • 134: PDI (Investigations Police).
  • 136: Andean Rescue Corps.
  • 137 and 138: maritime and air search and rescue.
  • 130: CONAF emergency number, per its official website.

Agencies: CONAF (national parks and wildfires), SERNATUR (tourism; it has a channel to complain about adventure tourism services at sernatur.cl/reclamos), SENAPRED (emergencies and disasters), Dirección Meteorológica de Chile and SERNAGEOMIN (volcanoes).

Frequently asked questions

Is Patagonia safe to visit?

Yes, with basic precautions. Canada asks for normal security precautions for Argentina, with regional advisories only for Greater Buenos Aires and Rosario, and the UK has no current warnings. The most common risks are theft from cars and bus terminals, changeable weather and the mountains.

Is Patagonia safe for solo travelers?

Official sources do not say you cannot, but Canada reports that women hiking and cycling alone have been assaulted in Argentina. The APN recommends always going with company on the trails around El Chaltén.

Are there dangerous animals in Patagonia?

Parks have big cats (puma). The APN says not to approach, to back away slowly, not to run and to tell the ranger. The most serious health risk in the region is hantavirus, which is carried by wild rodents.

What number do I call in an emergency in Patagonia?

In Argentina, 911 (national emergency center) and 106 for boating emergencies. In Chile, 131 (ambulance), 132 (fire), 133 (Carabineros) and 136 (Andean Rescue). In a park, use the emergency number the Visitor Center gives you.

Do I need health insurance to travel to Argentina?

National-government hospitals ask foreigners without permanent residence for health insurance or advance payment for non-emergency care (Resolution 1066/2026). Emergency care is never conditioned. We could not confirm that insurance is required on entry: check with Migraciones or a consulate. Traveling with insurance is recommended anyway.

When is it riskiest to travel in Patagonia?

In winter (May to September) trails around El Chaltén have ice, mud and snow, and in Los Glaciares studded tires or chains are mandatory when there is snow. Weather changes fast at any time of year, and wildfire risk rises in summer.
